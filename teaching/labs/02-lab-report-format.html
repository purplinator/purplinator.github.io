Every lab in this course is turned in the same way. Your group splits into three jobs, each job fills one whiteboard, and photos of the boards go into the Word template alongside your screenshots. The jobs are QPD (Question, Procedure, Diagram), UDA (Uncertainty, Data, Analysis) and CON (Conclusion). After “what uncertainty do I put here?”, the question I get most is “does this look okay?”, so this page is the answer: what belongs on each board, where it sits, and a checklist to run before anyone takes a photo.

The boards are about 75 cm by 50 cm. That is not much room, so what goes on one has to be chosen, and the layouts below are the ones that fit.

Who turns in what

NoteRegular labs and major labs
  • Regular labs. You turn in the part you worked on. In a group of two, one person does QPD, the other does UDA, both write CON, and each of you turns in your own part plus CON.
  • Major labs (Lab 5 and Lab 9). Every student turns in every part, and your grade depends on your partners’ work as much as your own. Check all three boards before any of them is photographed.
  • Lab 9 also has a theory session in the class period before the usual three-part lab.

Before you take the photo

Run this on every board, whatever the job. It takes thirty seconds and catches most of what I take points off for.

The QPD board

The test of a QPD board is simple: someone who has never seen the equipment should be able to read it, repeat your experiment, and know exactly which numbers you wrote down and in what units.

QPDYour name · partners' names
Q: What is the relationship between $x$ and $y$? (the real quantities, in words)
Procedure
  1. Numbered steps, in the order you did them.
  2. Every measured quantity named with its symbol and unit the first time it appears.
  3. What you held fixed, what you varied, how many trials.
  4. Where each number was read from (the Statistics box, the angle scale, the fit).
Diagram: an annotated photo in Word, or drawn here or on a second board

The QPD board. The header and the question take two lines; the procedure gets everything else.

  1. Header. Job label on the left, names on the right, a line under both.
  2. The question, written as a title under the header. It always has the form “What is the relationship between \(x\) and \(y\)?”, and \(x\) and \(y\) are the physical quantities in words: “the normal force on the block and the friction force between the block and the track”, not “\(F_N\) and \(f\)”. If the lab has more than one surface, spring or object, name them. The symbols can follow in brackets.
  3. The procedure, filling the rest of the board. Numbered steps in the order you did them, written for a reader who knows nothing about the lab. Name every quantity you measured, with its symbol and its unit, and say where the number came from: “read the maximum static friction force \(f_{s,\max}\) in newtons from the Statistics box over the peak.” Say what you held fixed and what you varied.
  4. The diagram. Most groups take a photo of the setup and annotate it in Word with arrows and labels on every measurable quantity: positions, distances, angles, which surface is which. Drawing it instead is fine, on a split board or a second one. Either way, “annotated” means labels. A photo with no labels is a photo, not a diagram.

The UDA board

The UDA job is two jobs. Most of it happens in Excel and never touches the board; the board is where you show the physics behind the spreadsheet.

In Excel, not on the board:

  • Data in the yellow cells, every calculated column completed, and both uncertainty rows for every column. The uncertainty guide walks through those two rows column by column.
  • The graph: the right quantities on the right axes, axis labels with units, one series per data set, a linear trendline on each series with its equation showing, and a legend that says which series is which.
  • Nearly every lab ends in a straight line, even when the relationship being tested is not linear, because we choose what to plot so that the line comes out straight and its slope means something. That graph is the linearized graph, and its trendline equation is the one the conclusion quotes.

On the board:

Sample calculations

One per calculated column, from a real row:

$M_\text{tot} = 0.25\,\text{kg} + 0.11\,\text{kg} = 0.36\,\text{kg}$

$F_N = (0.36\,\text{kg})(9.81\,\text{m/s}^2) = 3.5\,\text{N}$

… and so on down the calculated columns

The UDA board. Left half: where the formulas come from. Right half: proof that the spreadsheet uses them.

  1. Header, as before.
  2. Left half: the derivation. Whatever gets from the physics to the quantity on the graph. Often that is a free-body diagram, the force equations it implies, and the relationship that falls out; sometimes it is shorter. Label every force on the diagram and define every symbol.
  3. Right half: one sample calculation for every calculated column in the sheet. Pick a real row, write the measured numbers that feed it with their units, and show the arithmetic. One or two lines per column is enough. This is what proves the formula in the spreadsheet is the one from the physics, and it is where missing units get noticed, so put them in.

The CON board

One paragraph. The instructions for the conclusion arrive as a numbered list of four or five questions, and each question becomes exactly one sentence, in order. No bullet points, no headings, no “Q1:”. Someone reading only your paragraph should come away knowing what you found, how big it was, and how it compares with what was expected.

CONYour name · partners' names

1 The relationship between $x$ and $y$ is linear / quadratic / …. 2 The trendline equation(s), in the lab's symbols with units. 3 The result(s), with units and what they describe. 4 Sometimes: a comparison inside your own results. 5 The comparison to a reference value, as a percent difference.

one paragraph, one sentence per question, in order

The CON board. The numbers are here to show the order; they do not go on the board.

The sentences, one by one. Examples use the friction lab, with invented numbers.

  1. The answer to the lab question, in the words of the QPD question. “The relationship between the normal force on the block and the friction force on it is linear, for both static and kinetic friction.”
  2. The trendline equation, rewritten in the lab’s symbols with units. Excel gives you \(y = 0.314x + 0.02\). You write \(f_s = (0.314)\,F_N + 0.02\ \text{N}\), and you say that the coefficient is unitless because it is a force divided by a force. One equation per data set. If the relationship graph and the linearized graph are different graphs, quote both equations.
  3. The result, with units and with what it describes: “The coefficient of static friction between cork and poplar is 0.31.” All of them, if there are several.
  4. Sometimes, a comparison inside your results, when a question asks for it: “The static coefficient was larger than the kinetic coefficient for both surfaces.”
  5. The comparison to a reference: a known value, another measurement, or the class mean, usually as a percent difference. Use the formula the week’s instructions give; the two in circulation are \(\%\,\text{error} = 100\%\,(M - T)/T\) against a true value \(T\) and \(\%\,\text{difference} = 200\%\,(M_1 - M_2)/(M_1 + M_2)\) between two measurements.

Section What goes in it

CON Photo of the CON board.

Fill in the name, partners and section lines at the top. On a regular lab, delete the sections you did not do.

TipUnits, one more time

Before the photo, touch every number on the board and say its unit out loud. Then do the same for the equation. A slope has units: the slope of spring force in newtons against stretch in meters is in newtons per meter, which is exactly why it is a spring constant. A slope with no units is only unitless because both axes share a unit, and the coefficient of friction is the standard case, newtons over newtons. When that happens, say so in the sentence rather than leaving the reader to guess.
